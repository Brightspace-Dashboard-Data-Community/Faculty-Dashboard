1. Five rules of thumb

If you remember only this, you will stay aligned with the policy in most teaching situations.

  1. 1 You stay responsible. AI may assist. It may not replace your judgment. You own the syllabus, the grades, the emails, and the course content.
  2. 2 Do not paste student, employee, or college data into public AI tools. Names, IDs, grades, discussion posts, papers, emails, and rosters do not go into ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot (consumer), or similar open systems.
  3. 3 Tell students the rules for your course. Faculty choose Model A (no AI), Model B (AI with conditions), or Model C (AI built into the work). Put it in the syllabus and on assignments.
  4. 4 Review everything AI produces. Drafts, quiz questions, feedback comments, and summaries can be wrong, biased, or outdated. Edit before you use them.
  5. 5 Use college-approved tools for official work. Unapproved “shadow AI” is prohibited (see what that means). Complete college-approved AI training before using AI in your official duties.

2. Open vs. closed AI (why ChatGPT is different)

The policy treats public consumer tools differently from college-managed tools.

What this means

Open AI systems are public or consumer tools where what you type may be stored, reused, or used to train a model. Closed AI systems are institutionally managed or contractually restricted tools with defined privacy controls. Student work and other protected data may go into an AI system only when that system is college-approved and the use matches the college’s purpose.

Open tools (treat as public)

  • Consumer ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Copilot, or similar accounts you signed up for yourself
  • Free “summarize this PDF” or “grade this essay” websites
  • Anything you cannot confirm is college-approved

Closed / approved tools

  • AI features inside college-contracted systems after IT / SP2 review
  • Tools your division has been told are approved for faculty use
  • If you are unsure whether a tool is approved, do not put college data in it — ask IT or eLearning first

If the tool is on a public website and you logged in with a personal email, assume it is open. Do not paste student work there.

3. What you can use AI for

When it is consistent with this policy and other college rules, the policy lists these as acceptable — with human review every time.

What this means

AI is a drafting and thinking partner, not a substitute for you. Brainstorming, outlining, editing, research support, coding help, accessibility support, and analysis of non-sensitive data are allowed. You still verify facts, rewrite as needed, and take responsibility for what students see.

Examples that fit

  • “Give me 10 discussion prompts on photosynthesis for a 100-level course” — then you rewrite them to match your outcomes
  • “Tighten this announcement. Do not add new deadlines.” — you paste your draft, not student names
  • Asking a coding assistant why your HTML page will not indent a list
  • Using captioning, translation, or text-to-speech to make materials more accessible
  • Summarizing a public journal article you already have permission to use, then checking the summary against the original

Same idea, but not OK

  • Pasting a student’s discussion post and asking “what grade should this get?”
  • Uploading a class roster so AI can “draft personalized emails”
  • Publishing an AI-written module without reading it for errors or bias
  • Letting AI invent citations or textbook page numbers you did not verify
  • Using a free public tool to analyze quiz scores exported from Brightspace

4. What you must not do

The policy’s prohibited uses fall into a few buckets faculty hit most often: data, academic honesty, surveillance, automated decisions, and harmful content.

Student and college data (FERPA and privacy)

What this means

Do not enter personally identifiable student information, student work, employee records, financial data, or other restricted college data into public or non-approved AI. If you have to ask “would I put this on a postcard?”, do not put it in ChatGPT.

OK

  • “A student submitted a lab report that is mostly copied from the procedure. How can I write a rubric comment about originality?” — no names, no paper text
  • Redacting names and unique details before asking a general teaching question
  • Using an approved college system that already holds the student record

Not OK

  • Pasting “Jordan Lee, Colleague ID, missed Quiz 3, current grade 62%”
  • Uploading a student’s essay, discussion thread, or video transcript to a public AI to summarize or grade it
  • Dropping a grade export, classlist, or accommodation spreadsheet into any open AI tool

Academic misconduct (yours or a student’s)

What this means

AI may not be used to misrepresent work as original human effort. For students, that means following your course rules. For faculty, that means not treating AI text as if you wrote and verified it when you did not. Unauthorized or undisclosed AI use is academic misconduct.

OK

  • A Model B course allows outlining with AI if the student cites the tool and submits their own draft
  • You use AI to draft quiz distractors, then rewrite and check every item

Not OK

  • A student submits a ChatGPT essay in a Model A (no AI) course
  • You copy an AI lesson into Content and never read it
  • Using AI to skip the learning the assignment was designed to produce

Surveillance, ranking, and fully automated decisions

What this means

Do not use AI to track, profile, rank, or predict student or employee behavior without authorization. Do not fully automate admissions, grading, hiring, discipline, or financial aid. AI-generated evaluations need meaningful human review.

OK

  • You read every discussion post and use a rubric you control
  • Brightspace Class Progress shows activity; you decide who to email

Not OK

  • “Score these 40 posts and enter the grades” with no human review
  • An unapproved tool that ranks students as “at risk” from their writing style
  • Hidden AI monitoring of students without transparency

5. Syllabus models A, B, and C

Faculty have academic freedom to set AI expectations in their courses. The policy encourages you to pick a model and state it clearly in the syllabus and on assignments. Sample sentences below are starting points, not required wording.

Model A

AI prohibited

No generative AI on coursework. Any use is academic misconduct.

  • Best when the goal is to assess unaided writing, calculation, or studio work
  • Say so on the assignment, not only in the syllabus

Sample syllabus language Generative AI tools (ChatGPT and similar) may not be used to produce, outline, or revise work in this course. Submitting AI-generated work as your own is academic misconduct.

Model B

Allowed with conditions

AI is OK for named tasks only. Disclosure and citation are required.

  • Example: brainstorming and outlines yes; final draft no
  • Tell students exactly what to disclose (tool, purpose, how it helped)

Sample syllabus language You may use AI to brainstorm or outline. You may not use it to write the submitted draft. If you used AI, name the tool, what you asked it to do, and how you changed the output.

Model C

AI integrated

AI is part of the learning activity. Students document prompts, outputs, reflection, and verification.

  • Example: compare an AI summary to the reading, then write what it missed
  • Build the disclosure into the submission (not an afterthought)

Sample syllabus language This course uses AI as a learning tool. For each AI-supported assignment, submit your prompts, the output you used, what you verified or corrected, and a short reflection.

You can use different models for different assignments in the same course (for example, Model A on exams and Model B on a drafting workshop). Students must follow the rules you set for that task.

6. Grading and other high-impact decisions

Grading is a high-impact decision. The policy does not allow fully automated grading, admissions, hiring, discipline, or financial-aid decisions, or AI evaluations without meaningful human review.

What this means

You can use AI to help you think about feedback language or rubric wording. You cannot outsource the grade. If an AI tool suggests a score, you still read the work and you still enter the grade.

OK

  • You draft a comment bank, then personalize comments after reading each submission
  • Brightspace auto-grades a multiple-choice quiz you wrote and reviewed
  • You ask, without student text: “What are common gaps in intro-level lab conclusions?”

Not OK

  • Paste 30 papers into a public AI and import the scores it returns
  • Use an AI “risk score” as the only reason a student fails or is reported
  • Let a tool decide extra credit, incompletes, or academic dishonesty findings by itself

7. Disclosure and student work

When AI is used, the policy requires clear disclosure of the tool, the purpose, the kind of help it provided, and — when your course or the college requires it — key prompts that shaped the output.

What this means

If you allow AI, tell students how to show their work. If you use AI to create course materials or communications, follow any disclosure your supervisor, department, or the college asks for. Students remain responsible for accuracy and integrity of what they submit.

A useful student disclosure

  • Tool: ChatGPT
  • Purpose: generate three possible thesis statements
  • Help: I picked one idea, rewrote it, and wrote the paper myself
  • Prompt (if you require it): “Suggest thesis statements on X for a 2-page essay”

Not enough

  • “I used AI a little”
  • No tool named, no description of what it wrote vs. what the student wrote
  • Submitting the AI draft unchanged in a course that requires original work

8. AI detection tools

The policy allows cautious use of AI detectors. They may not be the sole basis for an academic misconduct finding because false positives are common.

What this means

A high “AI score” is a prompt to look closer — compare to the student’s other writing, talk with the student, review process work — not an automatic guilty finding. Do not paste the student’s paper into a random public detector (that is still putting student work into an open system).

  • Use only college-supported originality tools if you use detection at all.
  • Keep notes on why you believe misconduct occurred beyond a detector percentage.
  • Follow existing Student Code of Conduct processes. Do not improvise a penalty from a dashboard score.

9. Training, approved tools, and shadow AI

Employees must complete college-approved AI training before using AI in official duties. AI tools must be reviewed and approved by IT and/or the Technology Strategies, Priorities and Project Review committee (SP2). Shadow or unapproved AI is prohibited.

What is shadow AI?

Policy definition, in faculty language

Shadow AI is any AI tool, app, or model you use for Institution work without explicit approval, supervision, or security oversight from IT / cybersecurity. It is the AI version of installing software on your own because it looks useful — the college never reviewed it, does not have a contract for it, and cannot see how it handles data.

Open vs. closed (section 2) is about where the data goes. Shadow AI is about whether the college approved the tool for your job. A public ChatGPT account can be both open and shadow AI if you use it for official duties without approval.

This is shadow AI (prohibited)

  • Signing up for ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, or a “free AI grader” with your personal email and using it to write quizzes, announcements, feedback, or emails as part of your job
  • Installing an AI browser extension that summarizes discussions, emails, or student papers on a work computer
  • Requiring students to create accounts on an AI product IT has never reviewed
  • Sharing a department “unofficial” AI login that was never submitted to SP2
  • Pasting college work into a new AI website a colleague texted you, before anyone in IT has approved it

This is not shadow AI

  • Using AI features inside a college-contracted system (for example Brightspace or YuJa) after that feature has been approved
  • Using a tool your division has been told is approved, after you finish college-approved AI training
  • Asking IT / SP2 to review a product before you adopt it or require it of students
  • Personal, non-work use of an AI app on your own device with no student, employee, or college data

If you are not sure a tool is approved, treat it as unapproved. Do not put college work in it. Email IT or eLearning and ask before you use it for teaching.

  • Complete the college-approved AI training before you use AI for teaching, email, or other official work.
  • Do not require students to create accounts on unapproved AI products.
  • Vendor tools that use AI still need legal, privacy, security, and accessibility review before college use.

10. Common faculty scenarios

Short situations you are likely to hit this term.

I want help writing quiz questions

Ask an AI tool for draft items using your outcomes, with no student data. Then you rewrite, check answers, and remove biased or invented content.

Fits the policy — brainstorming and drafting with human review, as long as the tool use is otherwise allowed.

I have 40 discussion posts to grade this weekend

Do not paste the posts into ChatGPT. Grade in Brightspace with your rubric. You may ask a general question such as “What does a strong reply look like for this prompt?” without student text.

Does not fit if the AI sees student work in an open tool or assigns grades you do not review.

A student used ChatGPT on a paper

Check your syllabus model. In Model A, that can be misconduct. In Model B or C, check whether they disclosed as required. Do not rely only on a detector score. Follow Student Conduct processes.

Depends on your stated course rules — the policy backs those rules when you have made them clear.

I want AI captions or a transcript for a lecture video

Accessibility support such as captioning and transcription is listed as an acceptable use. Prefer college-supported tools (for example YuJa) so the media stays in an approved system. Always review captions for errors.

Fits the policy when you use approved tools and check the output.

A colleague sent me a free AI tool that “grades discussion posts in seconds”

That is shadow AI until IT / SP2 has approved it. Do not upload student posts, do not require the class to use it, and do not assume a colleague’s workflow is college-approved. Ask IT or eLearning to review the tool first.

Does not fit — unapproved tools used for official teaching work are prohibited, even if they seem efficient.

A student asks me to “just run my paper through ChatGPT and tell me if it’s good”

Decline to paste their paper into an open AI tool. Offer office-hours feedback, a rubric conference, or the Writing Center. Putting their work into a public model is a data problem even if they asked.

Do not paste the paper — student work is protected even when the student volunteers it.

11. Where to get help

Suspected misuse is reported to the appropriate office (Student Conduct, Human Resources, or IT). Enforcement follows existing disciplinary procedures.
